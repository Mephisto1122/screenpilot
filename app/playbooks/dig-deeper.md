When the obvious search gives nothing, follow a chain: what you find in one place becomes the search in the next.
- Pivot on identifiers: an exact name in quotes, a handle (the same one is often used on other platforms), a company number, a product code, a contact shown on a business page, a distinctive phrase from a text, a file name.
- Go where records are kept: company registries, patent and trademark databases, court and tender portals, app stores, domain records, standards bodies, library catalogues, statistics offices. Search them directly; web search often does not reach inside.
- Read the edges of a page: footer, "about", imprint, press, careers, the last page of a PDF, the photo credit, the small print under a table. Sources and names hide there.
- Turn it round: who links to this, who cites it, who sells it, who complains about it (reviews, forums, issue trackers).
- An image: search by image to see where it first appeared.
- A page that changed or vanished: web.archive.org has earlier versions; compare the dates.
- Another language or country version often has what the main one lacks.
- Write the chain down as you go (found A on page X, searched A, found B), so the result can be checked.
- Stop when two independent chains arrive at the same answer, or when three pivots in a row bring nothing new; then report what is known and what is not.
- This is for organisations, products, documents and public roles. Never use it to locate or profile a private person.
